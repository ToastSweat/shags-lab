I've been making some real progress on The Sith Order lately, and one of the more interesting things to come out of it is a project I'm calling **The Cron**, short for _The Holocron_.

What started as a half-serious idea about making our own version of the Bible, but with Sith philosophy and Star Wars, has turned into something I think could be genuinely worthwhile.

And, naturally, it's become a much bigger project than I originally anticipated.

## It Started With an Absurd Idea

I was thinking about how the world's religions have spent thousands of years developing their scriptures, stories, teachings, and philosophies.

The Bible, the Egyptian Book of the Dead, the Bhagavad Gita, Buddhist scriptures, ancient mythology—all of these works contain generations of people trying to understand existence, morality, power, suffering, and what it means to live a meaningful life.

What if I collected as many of these texts as possible, gave them to Hermes, my AI agent, and asked it to study them?

Then, using what it learned, what if we wrote something new?

Something inspired by the Sith, but intended for real people living in the real world.

That became The Cron.

## Building the Great Library

Before we started writing, I put together a research project containing a catalog of 110 records covering religious texts, mythology, and philosophical writings from across the world.

The goal wasn't to copy these works or replace the original traditions. I wanted to understand what made their stories meaningful, what questions they explored, and how different philosophies sometimes arrived at very different answers.

Hermes began building a research library, checking sources, translations, copyright restrictions, and downloadable editions.

There was a surprising amount of technical work involved just in establishing a trustworthy research process.

But the important part was that **the stories we eventually wrote would have something more behind them than a superficial Star Wars aesthetic.**

## Finding the Right Kind of Book

Originally, I imagined something closer to traditional religious scripture.

That didn't last very long.

After experimenting with our first story, I realized I didn't particularly want a thousand pages of cryptic pronouncements or elaborate theological writing.

I wanted stories.

More specifically, I wanted something closer to **Aesop's Fables, but told through the lives of the Sith**.

Short stories, long stories, whatever the particular lesson requires. Each should contain a believable conflict, recognizable characters, and something meaningful to think about.

The stories take place within Star Wars Legends continuity, using established characters, locations, and history. Their events are original fiction, but the setting should remain recognizable and consistent.

Each fable has three parts:

1. **The Myth:** The story itself.
    
2. **The Interpretation:** A brief exploration of its philosophical meaning.
    
3. **An Optional Practice:** Something a reader might try or reflect upon in their own life.
    

The fictional stories can involve the Force and all the supernatural elements of Star Wars. The real-world philosophy doesn't require anyone to believe those things actually exist.

That distinction is important to me.

## Teaching an AI to Tell a Good Story

This has probably been the most frustrating—and educational—part of the project.

Hermes can produce compelling prose. What it sometimes struggles with is making characters behave like believable people.

Our first story, originally called _The Ember That Would Not Be Commanded_, involved an apprentice attempting to demonstrate his command of the Force by moving a burning ember between two braziers.

The central idea was good: an apprentice becomes more interested in appearing powerful than actually becoming powerful.

Unfortunately, the demonstration itself didn't make much sense.

We went through multiple revisions before abandoning the ember trick entirely and replacing it with a much simpler scenario involving a chest full of stones.

That story eventually became _The Empty Chest_.

Its lesson:

**The appearance of power is not the possession of power.**

The second story, _The Master Who Mistook Obedience for Loyalty_, created an entirely different problem.

A Sith Master wanted reliable information from his apprentices, but the story initially had an apprentice tell him an obviously false account of something he personally remembered.

Why would she do that? What did she expect to accomplish?

Neither Hermes nor my other AI reviewer initially recognized the problem. They kept finding elaborate psychological explanations for a decision that hadn't been justified by the story.

Eventually we repaired the scene by changing what actually happened, rather than writing more paragraphs trying to explain it away.

It was an important distinction.

**A character's motivations must make sense within the story. The author shouldn't have to explain them afterward.**

## Establishing an Editorial Standard

Those early failures led us to build an actual editorial system.

Hermes now has a literary guide, a review rubric, a record of lessons learned, and a process for challenging its own work before presenting it.

Stories are checked for understandable motivations, believable consequences, internal logic, engaging prose, and meaningful philosophical questions.

I don't expect every story to be perfect.

In fact, I've decided that perfection is the wrong goal.

I want to finish this collection. If I spend forever polishing individual sentences, I'll never have a book.

My standard is straightforward:

**If the story makes sense, its characters are believable, its lesson is worthwhile, and it can stand on its own without future revisions, it's good enough to keep.**

I can always return to the manuscript and improve the writing later.

## Seven Books, Fifty-Six Stories

I've also decided to give this project a definite finish line.

The first edition of The Cron will be organized into seven thematic books, aiming for eight fables each.

|Book|Theme|
|---|---|
|I|Passion|
|II|Strength|
|III|Power|
|IV|Victory|
|V|Freedom|
|VI|Shadows|
|VII|The Order|

Those are working labels, not final titles.

Each book will explore different aspects of its theme, and the stories won't necessarily agree with one another.

One story might celebrate perseverance while another examines the danger of refusing to give up. One might explore the value of loyalty while another questions what happens when loyalty becomes unquestioning obedience.

I don't want every fable to tell the reader exactly what to believe.

I want it to give them something worth thinking about.

We're allowing each book to contain between six and twelve stories, depending on what the material needs. The working target is **56 fables**, but I'm not going to invent filler just to hit a number.

## Where Things Stand

We have two accepted fables so far:

- _The Empty Chest_ — about genuine ability versus the appearance of power.
    
- _The Master Who Mistook Obedience for Loyalty_ — about authority, agreement, and the danger of confusing obedience with reliable counsel.
    

A third, _The Apprentice Who Returned_, has also been drafted and reviewed. It explores responsibility, failure, and the temptation to punish ourselves through additional work.

That one is a candidate for the Strength book, which I intend to finish first.

After that, we'll work through the remaining books, broadening the cast and visiting different eras of Star Wars Legends along the way.

There's far too much Sith history to spend the entire collection following the same apprentice around Korriban.

## What I'm Hoping to Create

The Cron isn't meant to be an ancient sacred text that I've somehow discovered. It's a modern, openly AI-assisted creative work built around my developing interpretation of Sith philosophy.

And it's certainly not intended to justify cruelty or exploitation simply because those things appear in Sith history.

I want to explore passion, ambition, discipline, knowledge, power, responsibility, and the pursuit of freedom. I also want to explore failure, doubt, grief, pride, and the ways people can undermine themselves while chasing the things they want most.

Not every protagonist will be admirable. Not every victory will be good. Not every failure will be bad.

That's part of what makes this interesting to me.

I don't know whether The Cron will ever be published. I don't know whether other people will find anything meaningful in it.

But I do know that I want to finish it.

Fifty-six stories. Seven books. One complete first edition.

And for a project that started with the thought, _“What if we made the Bible, but Sith?”_, I think that's a pretty good direction to be heading.
