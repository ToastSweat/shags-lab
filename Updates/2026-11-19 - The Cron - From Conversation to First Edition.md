I now have a private first-edition manuscript of The Cron / The Holocron: forty-three fables across seven books, assembled and converted into a Word document. It came out at 202 pages in Word. What started as an experiment in writing Sith-themed philosophical fiction has acquired a contents page, editorial records and considerably more opinions about blank lines than I expected.

I worked with Hermes to develop, draft and review the material. The stories use Star Wars Legends as their fictional background, but the incidents and dialogue are our original fiction, not existing lore. The part I want to write about here is how we organized the work and kept deciding what belonged in it.

## A story before an answer

One of the most useful rules was also the simplest: a fable should give the reader something worth thinking about, rather than tell them what to think.

That changed what we checked. A character needed to want something, meet a credible obstacle and make a choice with consequences. If the story only worked after an explanation of its intended lesson, the scene still had work to do. Commentary was not allowed to supply a missing motive or make an unearned result convincing.

I also did not want seven collections of Sith making mistakes. Ambition, discovery, mastery and winning needed room to be worthwhile. A character could achieve something without the next paragraph arriving to explain why enjoying it was foolish.

## Make the decisions before making the pages

We put a proposal gate in front of each full story. Before drafting, we checked the cast and setting, the immediate want, the resistance, the important choice and what would actually follow from it. I approved the particular premise before the full prose was written.

We kept established Legends material separate from invented events, and bounded the research instead of treating every uncertainty as permission to open another biography. A useful source could establish a character's role without establishing a private friendship or an incident we wanted to invent.

The project also gained a writing guide, an editorial rubric, a workflow and a lessons register. Those held the decisions that otherwise would have been scattered through the conversation. I wanted the next first draft to benefit from earlier feedback, rather than require the same correction again with a different apprentice standing in the doorway.

## Give the reviewer less help

For story review, we used a separate reader context. The first stage presented the myth without its interpretation or the author's explanation of what was supposed to work. Only afterward did the reviewer see the fuller packet.

That gave us a first response to compare with the later analysis. An explanation might answer a question about the evidence. It could not retroactively make the initial reading more moving.

We asked for the three strongest objections and checked them against the actual text. Internal revision was limited to one targeted pass when needed, rather than an endless sequence of new critics and better-looking scores. Earlier drafts and adverse findings stayed in the project. There were process mistakes and qualified reviews; keeping those visible mattered more than making the history look tidy.

A separate AI review was still an AI review. It was useful resistance, not a certificate that a story was good. The final editorial decisions remained mine.

## Knowing when another story was worth writing

The working story count changed as we read each book as a collection. Reaching a number was not enough, and an empty allocation was not a reason to commission another fable.

There was one point where I pushed back on a recommendation to close a collection. The assessment identified building a new purpose after permanent personal loss as thinly covered. That sounded worth developing, so I asked us to explore it before settling the lineup.

That was a reason for more writing. Filling a vacant slot would not have been. We eventually settled on forty-three stories, approved their placement and order, and stopped treating the earlier targets as unfinished homework.

## Finish the manuscript without rewriting it

Once a story or book was accepted, its text stayed fixed. Assembly had a different job: put the accepted pieces in order, preserve the introductions and check that nothing had disappeared or been quietly changed.

Combining the books exposed shared navigation targets and an inherited separator newline around one introduction. We adjusted those presentation details in the combined copy, left the accepted originals alone and checked the copied text. A separate compilation audit then checked the complete assembled manuscript, its navigation and the new outer notes.

The Word conversion contributed a final nuisance. Word saved the document, then failed to reopen the same path until we supplied it in the native Windows form. The book existed; the path was having a disagreement with the software. We eventually reopened the saved file in Word and compared its text with the accepted manuscript, including all forty-three stories and seven introductions.

For now, I have an accepted private manuscript and a Word copy I can actually read. Final literary names can wait. The useful result is a finished checkpoint, with the earlier choices and reservations still attached, instead of another invitation to begin the drafting process again.
