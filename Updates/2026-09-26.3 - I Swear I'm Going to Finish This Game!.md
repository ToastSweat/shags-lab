I am making an actual Game Boy game.

Not a modern game that looks vaguely green.

Not a Unity game with a pixel filter.

An actual `.gb` ROM that runs on a Game Boy, is built with GBDK, has to fit into ROM banks, uses the Game Boy sound hardware, and can become a real cartridge if I ever finish the damn thing.

The game is called:

```
Garth's Game DMG
```

It is a virtual pet game about [[Garth Algar]].

Garth lives in a little industrial spaceship room, eats terrible food, plays games, sleeps, talks in a horrible little robot voice, tells jokes, and now has a growing gas problem that the player will have to manage.

Video games are art.

The project started much smaller than that.

The first goal was basically:

```
Put text on the screen.
```

I had a GBDK project in:

```text
C:\gbdk\examples\gb\garth_game_i
```

and I wanted to learn what I was doing instead of having an entire game dumped in front of me all at once.

So it started with a splash screen, some centered text, and a little menu.

Then I pressed Up and Down and the menu scrolled up the screen and duplicated itself.

Great start.

The problem was that I was clearing and redrawing the console like it was a normal terminal. The Game Boy did not agree with my interpretation of events.

That became one of the recurring themes of the project.

I would look at something and think:

> This should be simple.

And the Game Boy would respond:

> You have misunderstood tiles.

The splash screen briefly appeared scrambled while VRAM was loading, so I learned to turn the display off, load everything, and turn it back on.

I made food icons out of groups of 8×8 sprites and discovered that converted tiles do not necessarily stay in the order I imagined they would.

The food was jumbled.

Then it was differently jumbled.

Then pieces of the previous food stayed behind like some kind of haunted hamburger.

Eventually the food assets were split up, rebuilt, arranged into controlled quadrants, and made to behave.

That was around the point where this stopped being a test and started becoming a game.

Garth got three persistent needs:

```text
FOOD
FUN
POWER
```

They decrease over time.

The player maintains them by playing short minigames, and those minigames also feed a progression system that gradually raises the maximum value of each stat.

The starting cap is 30.

With enough activities, elapsed days, and good care, a stat can unlock another ten points at a time until it reaches 100.

When that happens the game displays:

```
MAXIMUM UP!
```

It also saves all of this to SRAM.

That meant adding CONTINUE, NEW GAME, save validation, and an overwrite warning so one careless button press did not murder Garth's entire history.

The first minigame was feeding him.

There are four food slots. Each slot cycles through pieces of ten different foods and the player stops them one at a time.

If all four match, it is Perfect.

Three and one is Tasty.

Two and two is Unique.

Everything else is Sloppy.

The game then invents a name for whatever biological incident the player created.

A perfect food is easy.

A mixed burger and pizza can become something like a Bizza or a Purgizza.

Failure can produce:

```
SLOPPY SNACK
```

which feels less like a result and more like a threat.

The second game became a button-matching game.

The third has the player move a little catcher across the bottom of the screen, catching falling Zs and avoiding exclamation points so Garth can get some sleep.

At this point the game had a real care loop.

Feed him.

Play with him.

Help him sleep.

Watch his mood change.

Save the game and come back later.

Then I taught him to talk.

I did not use recorded samples.

I made a tiny speech system called `Lang808` that breaks dialogue into letter-like sound tokens and plays them through the Game Boy audio hardware.

There are vowel sounds, consonant sounds, punctuation pauses, and timing rules.

It is not exactly speech.

It is the sound of a Game Boy being haunted by somebody who would like to tell you something.

Perfect for Garth.

His face animates while he talks, but that created another fun problem where changing the animation frame could restart the speech.

So he would begin a sentence, move his face, and apparently decide to begin the sentence again.

Later the sound could get stuck when leaving the screen, so the stop routine had to explicitly shut the channels down.

Then the dialogue grew large enough that it had to be split across ROM banks.

Then accessing the wrong thing from the wrong bank could produce a mostly white screen and trap me in TALK mode.

Normal game-development stuff.

Music came next.

The game now has menu music, four mood themes, three minigame songs, result music, synthesized speech, and sound effects.

The music uses hUGEDriver and lives in Bank 4.

The sound effects needed their own channel, which meant removing stray commands from that channel in the music exports.

And because the minigames use their own blocking loops, every one of those loops has to update the music driver itself or the song starts and then freezes.

That was a weird one.

The code says the music is playing.

The Game Boy says:

> No it isn't.

Memory has been an adventure too.

At one point ROM0 was 98% full with 341 bytes remaining.

Later banking work improved that, but the more recent builds still had ROM0 around 96–97% and the music bank around 90%.

Those are not numbers that inspire calm.

The Game Boy can absolutely do a lot, but it expects you to know exactly where everything lives.

Code can be in one bank.

Data can be in another.

The screen has its own tile memory.

Sprites have their own rules.

And if two screens both decide tile 211 belongs to them, the Game Boy does not schedule a meeting to resolve the conflict.

It simply corrupts one of them.

That happened with the randomized menu backgrounds. The menu looked fine until I visited the pet screen and came back, at which point some of the background styles were garbage.

The pet UI was overwriting part of the menu's tile range.

The fix was to reload all sixteen menu tiles every time the menu is shown.

Simple once you know what is happening.

Several hours less simple before that.

Eventually the game reached a point where the major systems worked.

The pet stats worked.

Saving worked.

Progression worked.

The three minigames worked.

Dialogue, face animation, speech, music, and sound effects worked.

I playtested it for a while and got feedback from other people.

And then I had the extremely convenient realization that I wanted to redesign almost every major screen in the game.

The current build is a good game-shaped machine.

The redesign is about making it look and feel like something I would actually want to buy.

The new home screen is a detailed little cabin.

Garth stands in the room and will have different animations depending on his mood.

Four stat bars sit across the top-right.

The bottom of the screen has icons for four minigames, a joke button, TALK mode, and a mood indicator.

And yes, there are now four stats.

```text
FOOD
FUN
POWER
GAS
```

GAS works backwards from the others.

FOOD, FUN, and POWER are better when they are high and slowly decrease over time.

GAS is better when it is low and slowly increases over time.

Eating will make it increase faster or give it an immediate bump.

Eventually Garth has to release the pressure.

Which brings us to the fourth minigame.

The existing games are also getting actual names and a shared three-part structure:

```text
TITLE ANIMATION
GAME
RESULTS
```

The food game is now:

```
FOOD IS GOOD
```

The button game is:

```
SIMON SAYS
```

The nap game remains:

```
CATCH SOME ZS
```

And the gas game is:

```
PASS THE GAS
```

The Pass the Gas title sequence currently goes:

```text
black screen
OOPSIE POOPSIE
black screen
WHOOPS POOPS
black screen
PASS THE GAS
```

This may be the best thing I have ever designed.

The play screen is an industrial pressure-control room with a huge bubbling tank in the center.

There are separate meters for burp pressure and fart pressure.

The controls are:

```text
A: BURP
B: FART
```

The exact game mechanic is still being worked out.

It may be a pressure-balancing game.

It may involve alternating the two releases.

It may involve some amount of button mashing.

The important thing is that the art has tremendous fart energy and I do not want to waste it on a bad game.

The other new screens have the same kind of oversized personality.

Food Is Good takes place inside a ridiculous food machine.

Simon Says has six large Game Boy button symbols and seven result lights.

Catch Some Zs now happens in space, with huge falling Zs, a bigger catcher, planets, stars, and the score shoved into the footer so the playfield can breathe.

The new images are mockups right now.

They still have to be broken into reusable 8×8 tiles, assigned to ROM banks, converted into Game Boy assets, and adjusted whenever the hardware tells me I have been too optimistic.

Some of them will change.

They have to.

But the direction finally feels right.

This is no longer just:

> Look, I made a virtual pet on a Game Boy.

I want it to become:

> Oh wow. I want that cartridge.

That is the finish line now.

A real ROM.

A real cartridge.

A label.

A box.

A manual.

Something that feels like it escaped from an alternate version of the 1990s where somebody approved a virtual pet game about a strange little man managing his food, mood, sleep, and internal gas pressure.

The current working game is the foundation.

The new home screen and minigames are the next enormous step.

I have already made the source archive, technical notes, game design document, roadmap, and enough documentation that Future Me should at least be able to identify the wreckage.

Now I just have to fit the new game into the Game Boy.

Easy.
